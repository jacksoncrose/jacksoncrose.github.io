## Problem

MSU Facilities has been converting campus exterior lighting from high
pressure sodium to LED since 2011, with goals around energy consumption,
fixture reliability, and pedestrian safety after dark. What they lacked was
a campus-wide picture of what that lighting produces on the
ground.

Night imagery is a hard case for the method that would produce that
picture. Photogrammetry software works by matching texture between frames,
and in imagery this dark most of the ground barely registers: the lit
paths carry detail the software can match, while the lawns between them
carry almost none.

## Approach

In September 2023 I flew a Vision Aerial Switchblade over the MSU campus
between 1:14 and 4:58 a.m., collecting 1,366 frames across 236 hectares at
roughly 123 meters above ground. The Sony a6000 ran with the exposure
locked at 1/60 second, ISO 12800, f/3.2, so every frame is directly
comparable to every other frame. No color balancing or
scene-adaptive normalization was applied at any stage, which is what lets brightness
on the finished sheets be read as a measurement.

Two things about how the survey was flown constrain everything downstream. The
flight ran as nine long north-south legs with no cross-hatch, which leaves
along-track drift with nothing to tie it against, and no ground control
(surveyed markers on the ground) was placed.

Facilities received a cartographic map of the campus lighting footprint,
composed in ArcGIS Pro. The comparison below is a separate pass over the
same frames: I rebuilt the dataset in Agisoft Metashape 1.8.4, Esri
Drone2Map 2026.1.1, and OpenDroneMap 3.6.0, comparing conventional
orthomosaics against conventional orthomosaics.

## What was delivered

Metashape aligned 1,309 of 1,370 frames, 95.5 percent. OpenDroneMap
reached 92.8 percent by keeping frames with as few as 22 observations.
Drone2Map topped out at 924 of 1,366, about 68 percent, across four
attempts at different settings; the roughly 440 frames it drops are the
dark ones with almost nothing to match, and no setting I tried recovered
them.

Metashape reconstructed 149 hectares across
both flown blocks. Drone2Map reconstructed 129 and never solved the south
block. OpenDroneMap produced one full-site mosaic that was unusable, and the
version that was usable had to be cropped to 71 hectares to fit in memory.

The first test was a flat unlit roof, 48.6 meters across, sampled through
the same code path in every product. Nothing on that surface emits light,
so anything above zero is the program's own behavior in shadow.

| Product | mean | median | P95 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Drone2Map | 3.44 | 1.71 | 5.29 |
| Metashape 2023 | 7.74 | 4.41 | 15.56 |
| OpenDroneMap, bounded rebuild | 1.92 | 1.18 | 3.29 |
| OpenDroneMap, first full-site build | 9.40 | 1.23 | 57.95 |

The second test was the whole overlap. Metashape and Drone2Map share 113.5
hectares. I resampled both into a common frame at 50 centimeters and
measured 4,540,751 paired samples, binning by Drone2Map's value so both
products are read on the same pixels rather than on their own.

|---|---|---|---|---|

### The color decision

Metashape's shadows carry a green cast, and a white balance was not an
option: in 84 percent of 4.7 million dark-ground pixels the blue channel
reads exactly zero, and a balance multiplies, so zero stays zero. Instead
each pixel was moved toward its own brightness by a fixed factor, which
leaves brightness itself unchanged. The largest deviation across the frame
is 0.667 of a digital number, which is 8-bit rounding.

covered both flown blocks. One sheet could not show both.

## Outcome

Facilities has a campus-wide view of what the conversion produces on the
ground: coverage, gaps, and spill, at a resolution where individual lamp
pools are legible.

With no ground control at 2 a.m., neither product is the reference. The
comparison identifies which failure mode each program has, not which one is
right.

Two failures cost the most time.
OpenDroneMap ran into memory limits at this scale: in 48 GB it would not
texture the full 280 hectare site above roughly 400,000 faces. Restricting
the reconstruction to 71 hectares let it texture 600,000 faces in thirteen
minutes, six times the mesh density of the full-site build. That fixed flat ground and did nothing for
trees, which still tear, because a 2.5D mesh cannot represent them at all.
Drone2Map's seamline blending, with color
balancing enabled, produced visibly worse output than leaving both switched
off: hard-edged seam polygons with parking stall lines running straight
across brightness jumps. Both work by reading the imagery around each
seam, and frames this dark give them almost nothing to read.

Every difficulty above comes back to how the survey was flown, not to the
processing. No setting in any of the three programs addresses drift down a
1.6 kilometer leg with no cross-strips to tie it against, so what I would
change is the flight: cross-hatch it, fly it with camera tilt, or place
ground control. The exposure is the other one. A single locked setting had
to cover ground where 22 percent of the reconstruction sits below brightness
3 while 16.6 percent of the brightest area runs above 240 out of 255; a
bracketed pass, or a slower shutter on a second circuit, would have bought
back both ends.
